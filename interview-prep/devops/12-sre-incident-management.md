# SRE & Incident Management — DevOps Interview Questions

---

### 1. What is Site Reliability Engineering, in one sentence, and how does it differ from traditional sysadmin/ops?
SRE is "what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations function" (Google's own framing) — applying software engineering rigor (automation, code review, measurable objectives, error budgets) to operations problems, rather than the traditional ops model of manual runbooks and reactive firefighting; SREs typically spend a defined portion of their time on actual software engineering (automation, tooling) rather than 100% on manual operational toil, and use error budgets/SLOs to make reliability-vs-velocity tradeoffs objective rather than purely political/subjective.

### 2. What is "toil," and why does SRE practice try to minimize it?
Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable operational work that scales linearly with service growth and provides no lasting engineering value (e.g., manually restarting a service every time it hits a known issue, instead of fixing the root cause or automating the restart). SRE practice caps how much toil a team should tolerate (Google's guideline is under 50% of an SRE's time) because unchecked toil crowds out the engineering work (automation, reliability improvements) that would actually reduce future toil — a team drowning in toil has no time to fix the reasons they're drowning in toil.

### 3. What are the phases of a typical incident response process?
**Detect** (alerting/monitoring notices an issue, ideally before customers report it). **Triage/declare** (assess severity, formally declare an incident, assign an Incident Commander). **Mitigate** (stop the bleeding — the fastest safe action to restore service, like a rollback, which is not necessarily the same as finding root cause yet). **Resolve** (confirm the mitigation actually worked and the incident is over). **Postmortem** (blameless retrospective — root cause analysis, timeline, and concrete follow-up action items to prevent recurrence).

### 4. What is an Incident Commander (IC), and why is having a single, clearly designated IC important during a major incident?
The IC is the single person responsible for coordinating the overall incident response — making final calls, managing communication, and delegating investigation/mitigation work to subject-matter experts — without necessarily being the one hands-on-keyboard debugging the issue themselves. It matters because during a chaotic, high-pressure incident with many people trying to help, having no single clear decision-maker leads to duplicated effort, conflicting actions (two people making opposite changes simultaneously), and unclear/delayed external communication — the IC role exists specifically to prevent the incident response itself from becoming part of the problem.

### 5. What is a blameless postmortem, and why is "blamelessness" a deliberate practice rather than just being nice?
A blameless postmortem focuses entirely on systemic/process causes of an incident (what conditions allowed this failure, what safeguards were missing, what made the mistake easy to make) rather than assigning individual fault ("who broke it"). It's deliberate because if engineers fear blame/punishment for incidents, they will hide information, be less forthcoming with the exact details needed for root-cause analysis, and become overly risk-averse (slowing down legitimate work) — a blameless culture makes people willing to fully disclose what actually happened, which is a prerequisite for actually fixing the underlying systemic issue rather than just replacing the "at-fault" person and leaving the same trap for the next engineer.

### 6. What should a good postmortem document contain?
A factual timeline (when the issue started, when it was detected, key actions taken and their timestamps, when it was resolved); the impact (user-facing impact, duration, affected metrics/SLO burn); root cause analysis (often via "5 Whys," digging past the immediate trigger to systemic contributing factors); what went well and what didn't in the response itself (not just the original bug); and concrete, assigned, tracked action items with owners and deadlines — a postmortem without action items that actually get done is just a story, not a reliability improvement.

### 7. Explain the "5 Whys" technique with a short example.
Repeatedly ask "why" to drill from a surface symptom down to a systemic root cause. Example: The site went down. Why? → The database ran out of connections. Why? → A new code path opened connections without closing them. Why? → It wasn't caught in code review or testing. Why? → There's no automated test or linting rule that catches connection leaks. Why? → The team hasn't invested in that tooling because no prior incident made the cost visible. The systemic fix (add connection-leak detection to CI, not just fixing that one code path) only emerges from going several levels deep, not stopping at the first "why."

### 8. What is MTTD, MTTR, MTBF, and MTTA, and why do teams track them?
**MTTD** (Mean Time To Detect) — how long from an issue starting to it being noticed. **MTTA** (Mean Time To Acknowledge) — how long from alert to a human acknowledging/starting to respond. **MTTR** (Mean Time To Resolve/Recover) — how long from detection to full resolution. **MTBF** (Mean Time Between Failures) — average time between incidents, a proxy for underlying system reliability. Teams track these to identify *where* in the incident lifecycle to invest improvement effort — e.g., a high MTTD with a low MTTR once detected points at monitoring/alerting gaps, not response-process problems, whereas the reverse points at runbook/tooling/process gaps.

### 9. What is on-call, and what practices reduce on-call burnout?
On-call is a rotation of engineers responsible for responding to production alerts/pages outside normal working hours. Practices that reduce burnout: actively tuning alerts to eliminate noise/non-actionable pages (see alert fatigue in the monitoring file), having a secondary/escalation on-call so a single person isn't the sole point of failure for coverage, fair and predictable rotation schedules with adequate rest between shifts, compensating on-call time appropriately, tracking and acting on pages-per-shift as a reliability metric in itself (a consistently overloaded rotation is a signal the underlying system needs reliability investment, not that the humans need to toughen up), and ensuring good runbooks/tooling so a page doesn't require deep tribal knowledge to act on.

### 10. What is a "game day" or chaos engineering exercise, and what's the goal?
A deliberately planned exercise where a team intentionally injects failure into a system (killing an instance, introducing network latency, simulating a dependency outage) in a controlled way — either in production (with careful blast-radius limits, as pioneered by Netflix's Chaos Monkey) or a close staging replica — to verify that assumed resilience mechanisms (failover, retries, circuit breakers, alerting) actually work as designed, and to build team familiarity/muscle memory with incident response *before* a real, uncontrolled incident forces it. The goal is finding weaknesses on your own terms, on a schedule, rather than discovering them for the first time during a real customer-impacting outage.

### 11. Explain a circuit breaker pattern and why it's used between services.
A circuit breaker wraps calls to a downstream dependency and tracks failure rates; if failures exceed a threshold, it "opens" — subsequent calls fail fast immediately (without even attempting the call) for a cooldown period, instead of continuing to send requests to (and pile up latency/resources waiting on) a service that's clearly already struggling. After the cooldown, it allows a small number of "trial" requests through ("half-open" state) to test if the dependency has recovered before fully closing again. This prevents cascading failure — a struggling downstream service being hammered with retries from every upstream caller, and upstream services themselves getting bogged down waiting on a doomed call, can bring down an otherwise-healthy system through simple resource exhaustion.

### 12. What is a "retry storm," and how do you prevent it?
When a dependency starts failing/slowing down, naive retry logic across many callers can multiply load on the already-struggling dependency (every failed request gets retried, sometimes multiple times, by potentially thousands of callers simultaneously) — making the underlying problem worse, sometimes turning a brief blip into a full outage. Prevention: exponential backoff with jitter (spread retries out over time and randomize them so they don't all hit again in lockstep), a retry budget/cap (limit total retries as a fraction of overall traffic, not per-request unbounded retries), and circuit breakers that stop retrying entirely once failure is clearly systemic rather than transient.

### 13. What is graceful degradation, and give an example.
Designing a system to continue providing reduced but still-useful functionality when a non-critical dependency fails, rather than failing the entire request/page. Example: an e-commerce product page whose "customers also bought" recommendations service is down should still render the core product info/purchase flow (skip or hide the recommendations section) rather than the whole page erroring out — the failure of a non-essential feature shouldn't take down an essential one.

### 14. What's the difference between a disaster recovery (DR) plan's RTO and RPO?
**RTO** (Recovery Time Objective) — the maximum acceptable *time* to restore service after a disaster (how long can you be down). **RPO** (Recovery Point Objective) — the maximum acceptable *amount of data loss*, measured in time (how much recent data can you afford to lose, i.e., how far back does your last usable backup/replica need to be). These two numbers drive concrete architecture decisions: a near-zero RPO requires synchronous replication (at a latency/cost tradeoff); a near-zero RTO requires hot standby infrastructure ready to take over instantly (at significant standing infrastructure cost) rather than restoring from backups after the fact.

### 15. Explain the difference between a hot, warm, and cold disaster recovery standby strategy.
**Cold** — backups exist, but standby infrastructure must be provisioned/restored from scratch when disaster strikes — cheapest, but slowest RTO (hours to days). **Warm** — a scaled-down but running standby environment exists, kept roughly in sync, that can be scaled up when needed — a middle ground on both cost and RTO. **Hot** — a fully provisioned, actively kept-in-sync standby environment ready to take over traffic immediately (often via automated failover) — fastest RTO (seconds to minutes) but the most expensive, since you're paying for essentially duplicate standing infrastructure that's idle until needed.

### 16. What is capacity planning, and what inputs go into it?
Capacity planning forecasts future resource needs (compute, storage, network, database connections) based on expected growth (organic traffic growth, planned marketing/product launches, seasonal patterns) combined with current headroom/utilization data, load testing results showing per-unit resource cost per unit of traffic, and lead time needed to actually provision additional capacity (which matters more for hardware/reserved capacity than for elastically auto-scaled cloud resources) — done proactively so scaling happens *ahead of* need rather than reactively during an already-degraded, high-traffic incident.

### 17. What is a status page, and what's the tradeoff in how much detail/speed to share externally during an ongoing incident?
A status page publicly communicates current service health/incidents to customers. The tradeoff: updating it quickly and transparently builds customer trust and reduces support-ticket volume (customers can self-serve "is it just me" answers), but posting before the situation and cause are well-understood risks publishing inaccurate information that has to be walked back, and overly technical detail can be more confusing than helpful (or, in some cases, reveal sensitive information about internal architecture to potential attackers) — most mature incident processes have a defined communications role (often distinct from the IC) responsible for calibrating what's shared externally and updating it at a steady cadence even when there's no new information ("still investigating" is itself valuable information).

### 18. How would you design an escalation policy for an on-call rotation, and why have multiple levels?
A typical escalation policy: an alert first pages the primary on-call; if unacknowledged within a defined window (e.g., 5 minutes), it escalates to a secondary on-call; if still unacknowledged, it escalates further to a team lead or triggers a broader notification. Multiple levels protect against a single point of human failure (the primary is asleep, has a dead phone, is unreachable) ensuring a real incident is never left completely unattended just because one specific person didn't respond — while the acknowledgment-timeout mechanism ensures escalation only happens when genuinely needed, not on every single page regardless of response time.
