# Microservices Architecture — Interview Questions

---

### 1. What is a microservices architecture, and what problem is it actually trying to solve (organizationally, not just technically)?
Microservices decompose an application into independently deployable services, each owning a specific business capability and (ideally) its own data. The core problem it solves is organizational scaling: as an engineering org grows, a single monolithic codebase becomes a bottleneck for many teams trying to develop, test, and deploy independently without stepping on each other (merge conflicts, coordinated release trains, one team's bug blocking everyone's deploy) — microservices let teams own, deploy, scale, and even choose the technology for their own service independently. It's fundamentally a solution to a *team/organizational* scaling problem (see Conway's Law) as much as, or more than, a purely technical one — which is why adopting it without the organizational scale/structure that motivates it often just adds distributed-systems complexity for no real benefit.

### 2. What is Conway's Law, and why does it matter when designing a microservices architecture?
"Organizations design systems that mirror their own communication structure." In practice, service boundaries tend to end up matching team boundaries whether or not that's deliberate — if you want a specific, clean service architecture, you often need to structure your teams to match it (or vice versa: your existing team structure will tend to produce a corresponding, possibly undesired, service structure regardless of the architecture diagram). This is why thoughtful microservices adoption usually involves deliberately designing team ownership boundaries alongside service boundaries, not just drawing box-and-arrow service diagrams in isolation from how the organization is actually structured.

### 3. What does "each service owns its own database" mean, and why is a shared database across microservices considered an anti-pattern?
Each service should be the only thing that directly reads/writes its own underlying data store; other services access that data only through the owning service's API, never by directly querying its database. A shared database is an anti-pattern because it creates hidden, uncontrolled coupling — any service can (and eventually will) come to depend on another service's internal table structure/schema, meaning the "owning" service can no longer freely change its own schema without risking breaking other services it doesn't even know are directly querying it — effectively recreating tight monolith-like coupling while paying all the operational overhead of running separate services.

### 4. What is the API Gateway pattern, and what responsibilities does it typically take on?
A single entry point that all external client requests pass through before being routed to the appropriate backend microservice(s). Typical responsibilities: authentication/authorization enforcement (centralizing this rather than duplicating it in every service), rate limiting, request routing (mapping public-facing paths to internal service addresses, which can change independently of the public contract), protocol translation, response aggregation (combining calls to multiple backend services into one client-facing response, sometimes called the "Backend for Frontend" pattern when tailored per client type), and centralized logging/metrics for all inbound traffic.

### 5. What is service discovery, and why is it needed in a microservices environment (as opposed to hardcoding IP addresses)?
In a dynamic environment (containers/instances constantly starting, stopping, scaling, being replaced with new IPs), hardcoding a downstream service's address is infeasible. Service discovery lets a service find the current, healthy location(s) of another service dynamically at runtime — via a service registry (Consul, etcd) that services register with on startup and query to find others, or via DNS-based discovery (Kubernetes' built-in Service DNS) — abstracting away the constantly-changing underlying instance topology behind a stable logical name.

### 6. What is a service mesh (Istio, Linkerd), and what problems does it solve that application code shouldn't have to?
A service mesh adds a dedicated infrastructure layer (typically a sidecar proxy alongside every service instance) that transparently handles cross-cutting service-to-service networking concerns: mTLS encryption between services, retries/timeouts/circuit breaking, fine-grained traffic routing (canary/A-B splitting at the network layer), and detailed observability (automatic distributed tracing/metrics for every inter-service call) — all without any of this logic being written into each individual service's application code (and thus without every team having to correctly reimplement resilience patterns like retries/circuit-breaking themselves, inconsistently, in whatever language/framework their service happens to use).

### 7. What's the tradeoff of adopting a service mesh — why doesn't every microservices architecture use one?
A service mesh adds real operational complexity and resource overhead (a sidecar proxy per service instance, consuming CPU/memory and adding a small latency hop to every call) and a significant new piece of critical infrastructure that itself needs to be operated, upgraded, and debugged (mesh-related issues can be genuinely tricky to diagnose, since they sit transparently "in between" services). For a smaller number of services, or an organization not yet experiencing significant pain from inconsistent cross-cutting networking concerns implemented ad hoc in each service, the operational cost of adopting a full service mesh often outweighs its benefits — it tends to pay off more clearly at larger scale (many services, many teams, genuine need for consistent policy enforcement across all of them).

### 8. How do you handle a scenario where Service A needs data that's owned by Service B, for every single request it handles (tight coupling risk)?
Options, each with tradeoffs: synchronous API call to Service B on every request (simplest, but couples A's availability/latency directly to B's — if B is slow or down, A degrades too); caching the needed data in A (with appropriate invalidation/TTL, decoupling A's request path from B's real-time availability, at the cost of potential staleness); or having A maintain its own local, denormalized copy of the specific data it needs, kept in sync via events published by B whenever the relevant data changes (a common CQRS-adjacent pattern — A subscribes to B's "changed" events and updates its own local copy, so its reads never need a live call to B at all). The right choice depends on how fresh the data needs to be and how much coupling to B's availability is acceptable for A's own reliability requirements.

### 9. What is the strangler fig pattern for migrating a monolith to microservices?
Incrementally extract functionality out of a monolith into new standalone services one piece at a time, routing an increasing share of traffic for that functionality to the new service (via a routing layer/proxy in front of both) while the rest of the monolith continues serving everything not yet migrated — over time, more and more functionality is "strangled" out of the monolith into new services until eventually little or nothing of the original monolith remains for that functionality. This avoids the high-risk "big bang" rewrite approach (attempting to build and cut over to an entirely new microservices architecture all at once), letting each extraction be validated independently in production before moving to the next piece.

### 10. How do you handle distributed tracing and debugging across many microservices when something goes wrong?
Propagate a shared trace/correlation ID across every service call in a request's chain (via HTTP headers, standardized ideally via W3C Trace Context), so every log line and trace span from every service involved in that one logical request can be correlated together. Use a distributed tracing backend (Jaeger, Zipkin, or a vendor APM) to visualize the entire request's path and timing across all services as a single trace/flamegraph, rather than manually cross-referencing separate logs from a dozen different services by timestamp alone (extremely tedious and error-prone at scale) — see the observability file for more on this.

### 11. What is the "fallacies of distributed computing," and name a few?
A set of false assumptions developers new to distributed systems commonly (and dangerously) make: "the network is reliable" (it isn't — calls fail, time out, or get partially delivered); "latency is zero" (a network call, even to a nearby service, is orders of magnitude slower than an in-process function call, and this compounds significantly if a request chain calls many services sequentially); "bandwidth is infinite"; "the network is secure"; "topology doesn't change"; "there is one administrator"; "transport cost is zero"; "the network is homogeneous." Designing microservices while implicitly assuming any of these leads to systems that work fine in a demo/low-load test but fail unpredictably in real production conditions.

### 12. Why does moving from a monolith to microservices often initially make a system *less* reliable, not more, if not done carefully?
A monolith's internal function calls are effectively "free" and can't (individually) fail due to network issues — replacing many of those in-process calls with network calls between services introduces a large number of new potential failure points (network partitions, timeouts, a downstream service being temporarily unavailable or slow) that simply didn't exist before. Without deliberately engineering resilience patterns for this new reality (timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers, graceful degradation, bulkheads to isolate failure), a request chain spanning many services is often *less* reliable overall than the equivalent monolith call chain, since the request's success now depends on the availability of every service in the chain (assuming synchronous calls), each with its own independent failure probability, compounding — reliability actually requires deliberate additional engineering investment when decomposing into microservices, not something that comes for free from the architecture itself.

### 13. What is the Bulkhead pattern, and where does the name come from?
Named after a ship's bulkheads — physical compartments that, if one floods, prevent the entire ship from sinking by containing the damage to that one section. In software, the Bulkhead pattern isolates resources (e.g., separate connection pools, separate thread pools) per downstream dependency, so that one failing/slow dependency exhausting its allocated resources doesn't also starve resources needed to call other, healthy dependencies — without bulkheading, a single slow downstream service can consume an entire shared thread pool waiting on it, indirectly breaking calls to completely unrelated, healthy services that happen to share that same pool.

### 14. How would you decide where to draw service boundaries when designing a new microservices system (Domain-Driven Design's "bounded context")?
Boundaries should align with business domain boundaries and team ownership ("bounded contexts" in DDD terms) rather than purely technical layers (e.g., not "a service for the database layer" and "a service for the business logic layer," which would just recreate tight coupling across a network boundary). Look for natural seams where data and behavior change together and are owned by a cohesive team/business capability (e.g., "Orders," "Inventory," "Payments," each with its own data and logic), and prefer boundaries that minimize the frequency of changes needing to span multiple services simultaneously — if two "services" almost always need to be changed together for any given feature, that's a signal they may actually belong together as one service, not two prematurely split ones.

### 15. What are the operational costs of microservices that teams often underestimate before adopting the architecture?
Vastly increased infrastructure/tooling requirements (service discovery, distributed tracing, centralized logging, a CI/CD pipeline per service rather than one, container orchestration); the need for strong automated testing and contract testing to have any confidence across many independently-deployed, independently-versioned services; significantly more complex local development (running/mocking a dozen dependent services to test one change, versus running one monolith locally); genuine on-call/debugging complexity (a bug can span multiple services, requiring correlating logs/traces across all of them); and the organizational overhead of maintaining clear ownership and communication across many small services/teams rather than one shared codebase where anyone can more easily see the whole picture — many organizations underestimate this and end up with the complexity of microservices without the discipline (strong contracts, ownership clarity, tooling investment) needed to actually benefit from it.
