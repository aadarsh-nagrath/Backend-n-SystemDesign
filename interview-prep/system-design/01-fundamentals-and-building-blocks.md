# System Design Fundamentals & Building Blocks — Interview Questions

---

### 1. How should you approach a system design interview question, step by step?
1) **Clarify requirements** — functional (what must the system do) and non-functional (scale: users/QPS/data volume; latency requirements; consistency vs. availability needs; read-heavy vs. write-heavy) — never start designing before this. 2) **Estimate scale** (back-of-envelope numbers for QPS, storage, bandwidth) to inform later decisions. 3) **Define the API/interface** at a high level. 4) **High-level architecture** — draw the major components and how they connect. 5) **Deep dive** into the 1-2 most interesting/critical components (usually where the interviewer steers you — data model, a specific hard scaling problem). 6) **Address bottlenecks and tradeoffs** explicitly — identify single points of failure, scaling limits, and discuss alternatives with their tradeoffs rather than presenting one design as if it were the only option.

### 2. What's the difference between functional and non-functional requirements, and why must you clarify both before designing?
Functional requirements describe *what* the system does (users can post messages, users can search for products). Non-functional requirements describe *how well* it must do it (latency targets, availability targets, expected scale, consistency requirements, security/compliance needs). Designing without clarifying non-functional requirements first is dangerous because the "right" architecture for 100 users is often completely wrong for 100 million users, and the right tradeoff between consistency and availability depends entirely on the specific use case (a chat app can tolerate different consistency behavior than a payment system) — these requirements should drive every subsequent design decision, not be an afterthought.

### 3. Walk through back-of-envelope estimation for a system with 10 million daily active users, each making an average of 20 requests/day.
Total requests/day = 10M × 20 = 200M. Average QPS = 200M / 86,400 seconds ≈ 2,315 QPS. Peak QPS is typically estimated as 2-5x average to account for non-uniform traffic (e.g., ~5,000-10,000 QPS at peak). This kind of estimate immediately tells you whether a single well-provisioned server could plausibly handle it (thousands of QPS is achievable for a simple, well-optimized service on modern hardware) or whether you clearly need horizontal scaling/load balancing from the start (tens of thousands+ QPS) — driving concrete decisions like whether a single database can handle the write load or sharding is needed from day one.

### 4. Why do system design interviews emphasize tradeoffs over "the right answer"?
Real system design almost never has a single objectively correct solution — every choice (SQL vs NoSQL, strong vs. eventual consistency, sync vs. async processing) trades one desirable property for another, and the "right" choice depends on the specific requirements/constraints/priorities of that system. Interviewers are evaluating whether you can identify these tradeoffs explicitly, reason about which side of a tradeoff matters more *given the stated requirements*, and justify your choice — not whether you memorized "the" architecture for a specific well-known system, since real interview follow-ups will probe exactly these tradeoff decisions.

### 5. What is vertical scaling vs. horizontal scaling, and what are the practical limits/considerations of each?
(See also backend fundamentals file.) Vertical scaling (bigger machine) is simpler — no application changes needed, and works until you hit the largest available machine size, at which point you're stuck; also a single larger machine remains a single point of failure. Horizontal scaling (more machines) requires the application to be designed for it (statelessness, or explicit data partitioning for stateful components like databases) but scales far further and improves fault tolerance (losing one of many machines is a smaller relative impact) — most large-scale system designs ultimately rely on horizontal scaling for the components that need to handle the largest load, using vertical scaling only as a simpler first lever for smaller-scale components.

### 6. What is a load balancer's role in a high-level system design, and what should you mention about it in an interview beyond "it balances load"?
Beyond distributing traffic across backend instances (see the networking file for algorithms), mention: health checking (automatically routing around unhealthy instances), and that the load balancer itself needs to not be a single point of failure (typically achieved via redundant load balancer instances behind a virtual/floating IP, or a managed cloud load balancer that's inherently highly available) — an interview answer that adds a load balancer but doesn't acknowledge it needs its own redundancy has just moved the single point of failure rather than eliminating it.

### 7. When should a system design use a message queue/async processing rather than direct synchronous calls between components?
When the work doesn't need to complete before responding to the original request (e.g., sending a notification, updating an analytics count, generating a thumbnail after an image upload) — deferring it lets the user-facing request return quickly while the actual work happens in the background at the system's own processing pace. Also use it to decouple a fast producer from a slower consumer (smoothing bursts, as discussed in the message queues file), or to fan out one event to multiple independent downstream consumers without the producer needing to know about all of them.

### 8. How do you decide between SQL and NoSQL when designing a new system in an interview?
Ask (out loud, to the interviewer, since this demonstrates the right thinking): does the data have complex relationships requiring joins/multi-entity transactions (favors SQL)? Is the schema well-understood and relatively stable, or highly variable/rapidly evolving (SQL vs. NoSQL document model)? What's the expected write scale, and does a single SQL primary's write throughput realistically suffice, or is horizontal write scaling needed from the start (favors NoSQL's typically easier horizontal partitioning, or a sharded SQL setup)? Are strong ACID transactional guarantees across multiple entities required (strongly favors SQL, or a NoSQL store with genuinely equivalent transaction support)? There's rarely a universally "correct" answer — the goal is showing you understand what each choice is actually optimizing for.

### 9. What is a Content Delivery Network's role in a system design, and when should you explicitly call it out as a component?
A CDN caches static (and sometimes dynamic, with careful cache-control) content at edge locations near users, reducing latency and origin load. Call it out explicitly whenever the system serves significant static assets (images, video, JS/CSS bundles) to a geographically distributed user base, or whenever a specific read-heavy, rarely-changing piece of data could be served from edge caching to dramatically reduce load on the origin — omitting a CDN in a design serving media/static content at scale is a common interview gap an interviewer will likely probe.

### 10. What is consistent hashing, and why is it used for distributing data/load across a dynamic set of servers (rather than simple modulo hashing)?
Simple modulo hashing (`hash(key) % N` servers) means that adding or removing even a single server changes the value of N, which reassigns *almost every key* to a different server — catastrophic for a cache (near-total cache miss storm) or a sharded database (massive data reshuffling) every time the cluster resizes. Consistent hashing maps both servers and keys onto the same conceptual hash ring; a key is assigned to the next server clockwise from its position on the ring — adding or removing one server only affects the keys that fall in that server's specific range of the ring, leaving the vast majority of key-to-server assignments unchanged, making cluster resizing far cheaper and less disruptive.

### 11. What is the "hot key" / "hot shard" problem, and how do you mitigate it in a system design?
Even with a generally good partitioning strategy, some individual keys (a viral post, a celebrity's profile, a popular product during a flash sale) can receive dramatically disproportionate traffic, overwhelming whatever single shard/server happens to hold that key regardless of how evenly the overall keyspace is distributed. Mitigations: application-level caching in front of the hot key (so most reads never even reach the shard); splitting/replicating a specific known-hot key's data across multiple shards deliberately (with reads distributed across the replicas and writes reconciled, if the access pattern allows); or, for write-heavy hot keys, batching/buffering writes before they hit the underlying store.

### 12. What is a single point of failure (SPOF), and how do you systematically find them when reviewing a system design?
A SPOF is any component whose failure alone would bring down the whole system (or a significant part of it) with no automatic failover. To find them systematically, walk through every component in your architecture diagram and ask "if this one thing died right now, what happens?" — a single database instance, a single load balancer instance, a single message queue broker, even a single DNS provider, are all classic SPOFs unless explicitly made redundant (replication, multiple availability zones, standby failover). A thorough system design answer proactively identifies and addresses these rather than waiting for the interviewer to ask "but what if X fails?"

### 13. What is the difference between a "pull" and "push" model for delivering updates to clients (e.g., a news feed, notifications)?
Pull (polling) — clients periodically ask the server "anything new for me?" — simple to implement, works with any client capability (no persistent connection needed), but adds latency (up to the polling interval) and wastes resources on empty-result polls when there's nothing new. Push — the server proactively sends updates to clients as they happen (via WebSockets, Server-Sent Events, or mobile push notifications) — near-instant delivery and no wasted polling, but requires maintaining persistent connections/subscription state for potentially millions of clients, adding real infrastructure complexity (connection state management, fan-out to many simultaneous subscribers) that a stateless polling model avoids entirely.

### 14. What is fan-out, and the tradeoff between "fan-out on write" and "fan-out on read" (e.g., for a social media feed)?
**Fan-out on write** — when a user posts, immediately push/write that post into every one of their followers' pre-computed feed lists — makes reading a feed extremely fast (just read the pre-computed list), but makes writing expensive/slow for users with huge numbers of followers (a celebrity's single post might need to fan out to millions of feed lists) and wastes work pre-computing feeds for followers who may never even check their feed. **Fan-out on read** — compute a user's feed on-demand at read time by querying/merging all the people they follow's recent posts — cheap, simple writes (post once, done), but reads become expensive (especially for a user following many people), since every feed view requires aggregating from many sources live. Real large-scale systems (Twitter is the canonical example) typically use a hybrid: fan-out on write for most users, but fan-out on read (or a special-cased approach) for accounts with extremely large follower counts, to avoid the "celebrity write storm" problem.

### 15. What is idempotency's role at a system design level, specifically regarding retries between distributed components?
Any two components communicating over a network must assume calls can fail, time out, or have an ambiguous outcome (did the request actually get processed before the connection dropped, or not?) — safe retry logic (which is essential for building a resilient distributed system at all) is only safe if the retried operation is idempotent; otherwise, naive retries risk duplicate side effects (see the backend fundamentals file's idempotency question). A thorough system design should call out which operations need explicit idempotency mechanisms (an idempotency key, a natural idempotent operation) specifically at every network boundary where retries are expected to happen — this is a strong signal of production-experience thinking that interviewers specifically look for.

### 16. How do you handle "what if this component fails" questions gracefully in a system design interview, structurally?
Structure your answer around: detection (how would you know it failed — health checks, monitoring), immediate impact (what breaks, and does it fail gracefully/degrade or fail hard), automatic mitigation (does the system self-heal — failover, retry, circuit breaker — or does it require manual intervention), and blast radius (does this failure stay contained, or does it cascade to other components) — walking through this structure for any component the interviewer probes demonstrates systematic resilience thinking rather than an ad hoc "well I guess it would just... break" answer.

### 17. What's the difference between latency and throughput, and why can optimizing for one sometimes hurt the other?
Latency is how long a single operation takes end to end. Throughput is how many operations the system can complete per unit time overall. They can trade off against each other: batching requests together (processing many at once) typically improves throughput (more total work done per unit time, amortizing fixed overhead across a batch) but increases latency for any individual request in the batch (it has to wait for the batch to fill/process) — a system optimized purely for maximum throughput (large batches, high utilization, deep queues) will generally have worse tail latency than one optimized purely for minimum per-request latency (immediate processing, no batching, possibly leaving some capacity idle) — the right balance depends on whether the specific use case is more latency-sensitive (interactive user-facing requests) or throughput-sensitive (bulk background processing).
