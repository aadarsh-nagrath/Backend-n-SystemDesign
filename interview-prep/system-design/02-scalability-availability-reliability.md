# Scalability, Availability & Reliability — System Design Interview Questions

---

### 1. What does "five nines" (99.999%) availability actually mean in downtime terms, and why does each additional nine get exponentially harder to achieve?
99.999% availability allows about 5.26 minutes of downtime per year (vs. ~8.76 hours/year for 99.9%, "three nines"). Each additional nine gets exponentially harder because you're squeezing out an order of magnitude more of the *remaining* failure budget — going from three nines to four nines requires addressing failure modes that only manifest rarely enough to have been acceptable before, and going from four to five nines typically requires eliminating essentially all single points of failure, sophisticated automated failover with sub-minute detection/recovery, and often geographic redundancy — the cost and engineering complexity required rises much faster than the linear-seeming difference in the percentage would suggest.

### 2. How do you calculate the availability of a system with two components in series vs. in parallel (redundant)?
Components in series (both must work for the system to work): combined availability = A₁ × A₂ (e.g., two components each at 99.9% in series ≈ 99.8% combined — availability degrades when you chain dependent components, since ANY of them failing brings down the whole chain). Components in parallel/redundant (system works if at least one works): combined availability = 1 − (1−A₁) × (1−A₂) (e.g., two components each at 99% in parallel ≈ 99.99% combined — redundancy dramatically improves availability, since ALL of them must fail simultaneously for the system to fail). This is the mathematical basis for why adding redundant, independent replicas of a component is one of the most effective ways to improve overall system availability, while adding more serially-dependent components (more services in a synchronous call chain) hurts it.

### 3. What is a Service Level Objective (SLO), and how should it directly drive architectural decisions?
(See also the SRE file.) An SLO like "99.95% of requests succeed within 300ms over a rolling 30 days" directly implies concrete architectural requirements: multi-instance redundancy for each critical component (single instances can't sustain that availability), a specific caching/data-access strategy sized to meet the latency target under expected load, and a monitoring/alerting setup fine-grained enough to detect and respond to degradation before it burns through the error budget — a system design interview answer that states a specific availability/latency target and then designs concretely toward it (rather than a vague "make it fast and reliable") demonstrates the kind of requirements-driven thinking interviewers look for.

### 4. Explain database replication topologies: single-primary (leader-follower), multi-primary, and leaderless, with tradeoffs.
**Single-primary** — all writes go through one primary, replicated to one or more read replicas; simple to reason about (no write conflicts possible), but the primary is both a write-throughput ceiling and (without fast automatic failover) a availability risk. **Multi-primary** — multiple nodes can each accept writes, replicating to each other; improves write availability/geographic write latency (writes can be accepted locally in multiple regions) but introduces the hard problem of conflict resolution when the same data is written differently on two primaries near-simultaneously (last-write-wins, custom merge logic, or CRDTs). **Leaderless** (e.g., Cassandra/DynamoDB-style) — any replica can accept a write, and reads/writes use quorum-based consistency (a write succeeds once acknowledged by W replicas, a read is considered valid once R replicas agree, with W+R > total replicas guaranteeing at least one overlapping replica sees both) — offers tunable consistency/availability tradeoffs per operation, at the cost of more complex operational reasoning about exactly what consistency level is being guaranteed for any given read.

### 5. How would you design a system to survive an entire data center/availability zone failure?
Deploy redundant instances of every stateless component across at least 2-3 availability zones, behind a load balancer that health-checks and routes only to healthy zones. For stateful components (databases), use synchronous or near-synchronous cross-AZ replication with automated failover (a standby in a different AZ can take over the primary role if the primary's AZ goes down) — the specific replication mode (sync vs async) determines whether you might lose the most recent few writes during a failover (see RPO in the SRE file) or accept added write latency to guarantee zero loss. For surviving a full *region* failure (not just an AZ), the same pattern extends across regions, but adds significantly more complexity/cost (cross-region replication latency, potential need for active-active vs. active-passive multi-region design, and typically an explicit business decision about whether that level of resilience is actually justified by the cost).

### 6. What's the difference between active-active and active-passive (failover) multi-region architecture?
Active-passive — one region actively serves all traffic; a second, standby region is kept in sync (replicated data) but idle until a failover is triggered, at which point traffic is redirected to it — simpler to reason about (no concurrent-write conflict issues, since only one region ever accepts writes at a time), but the standby region's capacity sits mostly unused/wasted until an actual failover event, and failover itself takes some time (detecting the failure, redirecting DNS/traffic, promoting the standby). Active-active — multiple regions simultaneously serve live traffic, each capable of accepting writes — better resource utilization and can offer lower latency to geographically distributed users (each served by their nearest region) with instant failover (the other region(s) are already serving traffic, nothing to "promote"), but requires solving multi-primary write conflict resolution (see Q4) and is substantially more complex to build and reason about correctly.

### 7. What is graceful degradation at a system design level, and how would you design it into, e.g., a product page that depends on a recommendations service and a reviews service?
Design each non-essential dependency's failure to fail gracefully independently rather than taking down the entire page/request — e.g., wrap each dependency call with a timeout and a fallback (an empty/cached/default result) so a slow or down recommendations service simply results in that section being hidden or showing cached/stale recommendations, rather than the whole product page failing to load at all. This requires explicitly identifying, upfront in the design, which dependencies are truly essential to the core functionality (must fail the whole request if unavailable) versus enhancing-but-optional (should degrade gracefully) — a distinction interviewers often specifically probe for.

### 8. How would you design rate limiting at the system/architecture level (not just the algorithm) for a public API serving many different clients?
Enforce rate limits as early as possible in the request path (at the API gateway/edge, before the request consumes any backend resources) rather than deep inside application logic, to actually protect backend capacity rather than just returning an error after the expensive work has already happened. Store rate-limit counters in a fast, shared store (Redis, typically) accessible by all gateway/edge instances, so the limit is enforced consistently regardless of which specific instance handles a given request (a per-instance-only counter would let a client exceed the intended global limit simply by having requests happen to spread across multiple instances). Consider tiered limits (different limits per API key/plan tier) and communicate limit status via response headers so well-behaved clients can self-regulate.

### 9. What is the difference between horizontal partitioning (sharding) for scale and replication for availability, and why do large systems typically need both simultaneously?
Sharding splits data across multiple nodes so no single node needs to hold/serve the entire dataset — solves a *scale* problem (more data/writes than one node can handle). Replication keeps multiple copies of the *same* data across nodes — solves an *availability* problem (losing one node doesn't lose that data) and can also help read scale. A large system typically needs both simultaneously: shard the data to scale beyond one node's capacity, and *also* replicate each individual shard (so each shard itself has redundant copies) — losing a single node in a sharded-but-unreplicated system would mean permanently losing that shard's entire slice of data, so sharding without replication trades a scale problem for a new, serious availability/durability risk.

### 10. What does "designing for failure" mean as a system design philosophy, and how does it differ from trying to prevent all failures?
Rather than assuming (or trying to guarantee) that no component will ever fail — an assumption that becomes increasingly unrealistic as a system grows to include more components, each with its own independent failure probability, compounding — "design for failure" assumes failures of individual components *will* happen regularly (a server will crash, a network call will time out, a disk will fail) as a normal, expected operating condition, and designs the system to detect and route around/recover from these failures automatically rather than needing every individual component to be perfectly reliable. This philosophy underlies practices like automatic failover, retries with backoff, circuit breakers, and redundancy — accepting that any individual piece can and will fail, while engineering the overall system to remain reliable despite that.

### 11. How would you design a system to gracefully handle a sudden 100x traffic spike (e.g., a viral event)?
Auto-scaling for stateless compute tiers (with pre-warmed capacity or fast-scaling triggers, since reactive scaling alone may be too slow for a truly sudden spike — some systems pre-provision headroom for known high-risk events). Aggressive caching/CDN usage to absorb read traffic without it reaching the origin/database at all. A message queue buffer in front of anything that can be processed asynchronously, so write/processing spikes are smoothed rather than directly overwhelming backend capacity (see the message queues file's flash-sale question). Load shedding/graceful degradation as a last resort (deliberately rejecting or degrading lower-priority requests to preserve capacity for core functionality, rather than the whole system collapsing under load it genuinely cannot handle) — a mature design acknowledges that at some point, *some* explicit, controlled degradation strategy is better than an uncontrolled crash.

### 12. What's the difference between a "warm" cache/system and a "cold start" problem at scale, and how does this affect failover/scaling design?
A newly started instance (a freshly launched server, a freshly failed-over database replica now serving as primary, a freshly deployed cache) starts "cold" — with no cached data yet, and often not having received traffic/load ramped up gradually, so it hasn't yet reached its steady-state, most-efficient operating condition. This matters because a naive failover/auto-scale-up event can send full production load immediately to a cold instance/replica that isn't actually ready to handle it efficiently yet (a cold cache means every request briefly becomes a cache miss hitting the origin/database hard, potentially overwhelming it right when it's already under stress from whatever triggered the failover/scale event in the first place) — mature designs account for this with gradual traffic ramp-up onto newly scaled/failed-over instances, and/or pre-warming strategies (proactively populating a cache before it starts receiving real traffic) where feasible.
